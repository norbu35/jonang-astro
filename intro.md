The Luminous Path of Other-Emptiness: A Comprehensive Guide to the Jonang Tradition of Tibetan BuddhismIntroduction to Buddhist FundamentalsTo comprehend the specialized heritage of the Jonang tradition, one must first locate it within the broader framework of Buddhist thought. Buddhism originated in the 6th to 5th centuries BCE through the awakening and teachings of Siddhārtha Gautama, known as the historical Buddha Shakyamuni. Central to all Buddhist traditions are the Four Noble Truths: the truth of suffering (duḥkha), the origin of suffering (samudaya) rooted in ignorance and craving, the cessation of suffering (nirodha) known as nirvāṇa, and the path (mārga) leading to cessation, traditionally framed as the Noble Eightfold Path.All classical Buddhist philosophy rests upon the Three Marks of Existence (trilakṣaṇa): impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (duḥkha), and non-self (anātman). The doctrine of non-self asserts that living beings possess no permanent, unchanging soul or essence. Instead, individual existence consists of five transient aggregates (skandhas): form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. The continuity of existence across lives is governed by pratītyasamutpāda (dependent origination)—the principle that all physical and mental phenomena arise in dependence upon causes and conditions, lacking independent, autonomous existence.As Buddhism expanded across Asia, it developed three major vehicles (yānas), each tailored to different spiritual capacities and objectives:VehiclePrimary IdealCore Method & FocusPhilosophical EmphasisTheravāda (Way of the Elders)Arhat (Individual Liberation)Monastic discipline (Vinaya), analytical mindfulness (Vipassanā)Analysis of aggregates, elimination of mental defilementsMahāyāna (Great Vehicle)Bodhisattva (Universal Liberation)Cultivation of altruistic intention (Bodhicitta), Six Perfections (Pāramitās)Emptiness (Śūnyatā), inherent Buddha-nature (Tathāgatagarbha)Vajrayāna (Diamond/Tantric Vehicle)Siddha / Fully Awakened BuddhaEsoteric ritual, deity visualization, subtle energy yogas, direct empowermentSwift transformation of delusion into wisdom within a single lifetimeMahāyāna hermeneutics organizes the Buddha’s vast teaching career through the doctrine of the Three Turnings of the Wheel of Dharma (Dharmacakrapravartana). The First Turning, delivered at Sār Nāth, focused on the Four Noble Truths, personal liberation, and the lack of a personal self (pudgala-nairātmya). The Second Turning, delivered at Vulture Peak, focused on the Perfection of Wisdom (Prajñāpāramitā) sutras, establishing that all phenomena are empty of inherent existence (dharma-nairātmya). The Third Turning, delivered in various locations such as Vaiśālī, focused on the absolute nature of mind, primordial awareness (jñāna), and the latent essence of Buddhahood (Tathāgatagarbha) present in all sentient beings. This framework of the Three Turnings serves as the foundational battlefield for Tibetan scholastic debates, directly informing how the Jonang tradition interprets reality.The Landscape of Tibetan BuddhismBuddhism entered Tibet in two historical waves: the Early Propagation (Nyingma or Ngangdar) during the 8th century under King Trisong Detsen, Padmasambhava, and Śāntarakṣita; and the Later Propagation (Sarma or "New Translation" schools) beginning in the late 10th century. The Sarma movements drew fresh Sanskrit translations from Indian universities such as Nālandā and Vikramaśīla, establishing rigorous monastic curricula and complex tantric practice lines.Tibetan Buddhism integrates the philosophical foundation of Mahāyāna with the transformative methods of Vajrayāna. It places ultimate importance on the spiritual preceptor (guru or lama), who bestows tantric empowerments (abhiṣeka), oral instructions (tri), and ritual transmissions (lung). Practice is broadly divided into Sutra (analytical study of philosophy) and Tantra (contemplative methods operating on the subtle body's channels, energy-winds, and essences).The primary historical branches of Indo-Tibetan Buddhism emerged through distinct translation lines and transmission lineages. The Nyingma tradition represents the Early Propagation, while the Sarma traditions—comprising Sakya, Kagyu, Gelug, and Jonang—developed during the Later Propagation.Historically, Tibetan religious life consolidated into five principal living lineages, each maintaining distinct institutional centers, textual canons, and meditative specialties:Nyingma: The "Ancient School," centered on the Terma (revealed treasure texts) transmission and the non-dual meditation system of Dzogchen (Great Perfection).Kagyu: The "Oral Lineage," descending from Indian mahāsiddhas Tilopa and Nāropa via Marpa, Milarepa, and Gampopa, specializing in Mahāmudrā (Great Seal) and the Six Yogas of Nāropa.Sakya: Founded by the Khön family, renowned for systematic scholasticism, the Lamdré (Path and Fruit) system, and the preservation of the Hevajra Tantra.Gelug: The "Virtuous School," established in the 15th century by Je Tsongkhapa, emphasizing strict monastic discipline, rigorous debate, and Prāsaṅgika Mādhyamika philosophy.Jonang: The specialized tradition upholding the Drō lineage of the Kālacakra Tantra, the Six-fold Vajrayoga of the completion stage, and the Shentong ("other-emptiness") view of Great Mādhyamaka.Alongside these five Buddhist schools stands Bön, Tibet's indigenous spiritual tradition, which evolved alongside Tibetan Buddhism while sharing similar monastic and ritual forms.Genesis and Historical Evolution of the Jonang TraditionThe roots of the Jonang school trace back to 11th-century India and Kashmir. The Kashmiri scholar Somanātha traveled to Tibet in the early 11th century, translating the Kālacakra Tantra and its primary commentary, the Vimalaprabhā, alongside the Tibetan translator Drotön Sherab Drak. This partnership established the Drō transmission lineage of Kālacakra in Tibet.The pivotal early Tibetan figure in this lineage was the 11th-century yogi Yumo Mikyö Dorjé (b. 1027). A direct student of Somanātha, Yumo Mikyö Dorjé engaged in deep meditative retreats on the Kālacakra completion stage, during which he experienced realizations regarding the nature of mind. He became the first Tibetan master to explicitly articulate what would later be termed "tantric Shentong"—the view that ultimate reality is an absolute, radiant awareness empty of adventitious defilements.In the late 13th century, the master Kunpang Thukje Tsöndrü (1243–1313) accomplished a massive synthesis. He collected seventeen distinct transmission streams of the Kālacakra completion stage yogas circulating across Tibet and merged them into a single, highly coherent system. Kunpang-pa established a mountain retreat center in the Jomonang Valley of the Tsang region in Central Tibet, from which the lineage derived its name: Jonang.The intellectual and spiritual summit of the Jonang tradition was achieved under Dölpopa Sherab Gyaltsen (1292–1361), frequently referred to as "The Omniscient Buddha from Dölpo". Dölpopa received a comprehensive education across the Sakya, Kagyu, and Nyingma traditions. In 1321, he visited Jomonang Monastery, where he undertook intense retreats under Khetsun Yönten Gyatso, realizing the deep unity between the Kālacakra completion stage and the ultimate meaning of the Third Turning sutras.Dölpopa assumed the throne of Jonang Monastery and undertook the monumental construction of the Jonang Kumbum, a massive multi-tiered stupa designed in accord with Kālacakra cosmology. During the stupa's construction in 1333, Dölpopa formally broke with the prevailing intellectual orthodoxy of his day by publicly teaching Shentong (Other-Emptiness). He codified this realization in his magnum opus, Mountain Dharma: An Ocean of Definitive Meaning (Ri chos nges don rgya mtsho). In this work, Dölpopa drew upon Indian Buddhist scriptures to argue that while conventional reality is empty of self-nature (rangtong), ultimate reality (Dharmatā or Tathāgatagarbha) is empty only of relative, adventitious flaws (shentong).Centuries later, the tradition produced another monumental scholar: Jetsun Tārānātha (1575–1634). Recognized as the 16th throne-holder of Jonang, Tārānātha was an exceptional Sanskritist, historian, and tantric master. His History of Buddhism in India (1608) remains a primary source for modern Indian and Tibetan historiography. Tārānātha founded the major monastic center of Takten Damchöling in the Tsangpo Valley and authored definitive practice manuals for the Kālacakra generation and completion stages that remain standard across lineages today.Doctrinal Core: The Shentong Philosophy vs. RangtongAt the heart of the Jonang tradition's distinct identity is its philosophical stance on the nature of emptiness (śūnyatā) and ultimate truth, expressed through the contrast between Rangtong ("Self-Emptiness") and Shentong ("Other-Emptiness").The analysis of reality in Jonang philosophy divides existence into two distinct truths. Relative reality (conventional phenomena) is classified as Rangtong ("Empty of Self"), meaning it lacks inherent substance, is deceptive, contingent, and impermanent. Ultimate reality (the absolute ground) is classified as Shentong ("Empty of Other"), meaning it is devoid of adventitious defilements and conceptual fabrications while inherently possessing radiant, unconditioned Buddha-qualities.The standard Rangtong view, championed primarily by the Gelug school and rooted in the Prāsaṅgika Mādhyamika of Candrakīrti, asserts that all phenomena—both relative objects and ultimate truths—are empty of inherent existence (svabhāva). From the Rangtong perspective, emptiness is defined as a non-affirming negative (prasajya-pratiṣedha): the mere absence or negation of inherent existence, without implying any positive entity, ground, or underlying substance behind the negation.Conversely, the Jonang school's Shentong view asserts that reality must be analyzed through a dual framework that distinguishes the relative from the ultimate:The Relative is Rangtong: All conventional, dependent phenomena (paratantra) and conceptual fabrications (parikalpita) are empty of themselves (rangtong). They are impermanent, contingent, deceptive, and completely devoid of inherent substance.The Ultimate is Shentong: Ultimate reality (yoniṣpanna), identified as Buddha-nature (Tathāgatagarbha), primordial wisdom (jñāna), and the clear-light nature of mind, is empty of everything other than itself (gzhan-stong). It is completely empty of adventitious defilements, dualistic concepts, and deceptive phenomena. However, it is not empty of its own intrinsic, unconditioned Buddha-qualities—such as omniscience, compassion, and bliss—which are spontaneously present (lhun grub) and eternal.Philosophical AxisRangtong (Self-Emptiness)Shentong (Other-Emptiness)Primary Institutional HomeGelug (and scholastic branches of Sakya)Jonang (with Karma Kagyu and Nyingma variations)Definition of EmptinessAbsolute negation of inherent existence across all phenomenaNegation of adventitious defilements from ultimate realityStatus of Ultimate RealityUnconstructed absence of intrinsic nature; non-affirming negativeNon-dual, unconditioned primordial awareness (jñāna) endowed with qualitiesScriptural AuthoritySecond Turning (Prajñāpāramitā Sutras, Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā)Third Turning (Tathāgatagarbha Sutras, Maitreya's Uttaratantra)Hermeneutical StanceSecond Turning is definitive (nītārtha); Third Turning is provisionalThird Turning is definitive (nītārtha); Second Turning is provisionalDölpopa structured this stance by analyzing the Third Turning sutras, citing ten specific scriptures as definitive texts (nītārtha):Tathāgatagarbha SūtraMahāyāna Mahāparinirvāṇa SūtraŚrīmālādevī Siṃhanāda SūtraAṅgulimālīya SūtraMahābherīharaka SūtraAvikalpapraveśa DhāraṇīŚūnyatānāma MahāsūtraTathāgatamahākaruṇānirdeśa SūtraTathāgataguṇajñānācintyaviṣayāvatāranirdeśa SūtraMahāmegha Sūtra[cite: 11]To justify Shentong, Dölpopa integrated Yogācāra terminology—specifically the Three Natures (trisvabhāva): the conceptualized (parikalpita), the dependent (paratantra), and the consummate (parinishpanna)—with Mādhyamika dialectic, creating what he termed Mahā-Mādhyamaka (Great Middle Way). He maintained that if ultimate reality were merely a non-affirming negation without positive unconditioned qualities, liberation would be a state of total extinction, rendering the spontaneous activities of enlightened Buddhas impossible.Critics of Shentong, particularly Gelug scholars, argued that describing Buddha-nature as an eternal, inherently existent substratum risked reintroducing Hindu concepts of a permanent self (ātman). Dölpopa countered by stating that Buddha-nature is devoid of any personal ego or conceptual self-nature. He asserted that terms like "True Self" (paramātman) used in the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra refer specifically to the complete transcendence of all conceptual limitations and dualistic suffering.Practical Engine: The Kalachakra Tantra and the Six Vajra YogasWhile Shentong provides the philosophical ground, the Kālacakra Tantra ("Wheel of Time") serves as the contemplative engine of the Jonang tradition. According to tantric history, Buddha Shakyamuni revealed the Kālacakra Tantra at the stupa of Dhānyakaṭaka in southern India upon the request of King Suchandra of Shambhala. The teachings were preserved in the mystical realm of Shambhala by a succession of Dharma Kings and Kalki rulers before returning to India in the 10th century.The Kālacakra system organizes reality into three interrelated dimensions:External Kālacakra: The physical universe, cosmic cycles, planetary movements, calendar systems, and elements.Internal Kālacakra: The human psychophysiology, comprising the subtle body's channels (nāḍī), energy-winds (prāṇa), drops (bindu), and biological cycles.Alternative Kālacakra: The spiritual path, consisting of empowerments, generation stage visualizations, and completion stage yogas designed to purify external and internal realities, manifesting the ultimate Buddha Body (Adibuddha).While other Tibetan schools transmitted the Rwa lineage of Kālacakra (which emphasizes generation stage practice and empowerments), Jonang became the exclusive guardian of the completion stage practices of the Drō lineage. This completion stage is structured as the Six-fold Vajrayoga (Ṣaḍaṅgayoga):Yoga of Withdrawal (So-sor sdud-pa / Pratyāhāra): The practitioner retracts sensory awareness from external objects, directing attention into the central channel (avadhūti). This causes the initial manifestations of empty-form (śūnyatā-bimba) to appear in dark retreat, experienced as night-like signs (smoke, mirages, fireflies, lamp lights) and day-like signs (sunlight, moonbeams, lightning, drops).Yoga of Meditative Concentration (Bsam-gtan / Dhyāna): The practitioner stabilizes the empty-form visions, integrating them with non-conceptual awareness (prajñā) to develop deep meditative insight.Yoga of Harnessing Life-Force (Srog-rtsol / Prāṇāyāma): Focuses on controlling subtle energy-winds (prāṇa) through breath containment techniques (kumbhaka), drawing the upper and lower winds into the central channel at the navel chakra.Yoga of Retention ('Dzin-pa / Dhāraṇā): The energy-winds and subtle drops (bindu) are held bound within the central channel, dissolving dualistic concepts and generating immutable bliss (akṣarasukha).Yoga of Recollection (Rjes-sbran / Anusmṛti): The inner fire (caṇḍālī or tummo) blazes upward through the central channel, melting the subtle drops at the crown chakra. The practitioner directly perceives the luminous empty-form mandala of Kālacakra.Yoga of Meditative Stabilization (Nging-nge-'dzin / Samādhi): The complete union of unchanging supreme bliss (paramākṣarasukha) and the ultimate empty-form body (stong-gzugs). The physical body of karmic energy is completely consumed, revealing the fully enlightened state of the Primordial Buddha (Adibuddha).In contrast to systems like Guhyasamāja, which construct an "illusory body" (sgyu-lus) during post-meditation periods, the Kālacakra system generates a "devoid-form body" (stong-gzugs) directly during meditative equipoise (samāhita) on other-emptiness. This devoid-form body serves as the immediate cause for manifesting a Buddha's Form Body (Rūpakāya).Crisis, Suppression, and ResurgenceThe Jonang school flourished as a dominant intellectual force across Central Tibet from the 14th through the early 17th centuries. However, this period came to a sudden end due to political instability during the mid-17th century.During the 17th century, Tibet was engulfed in civil war. The local rulers of the Tsang province (the Tsangpa dynasty) fought for dominance against the rising power of the Gelug school in Ü (Lhasa). The Jonang tradition, along with branches of the Kagyu school, maintained political ties with the Tsangpa king. When the Fifth Dalai Lama, Ngawang Lobsang Gyatso, formed an alliance with the Mongol leader Güshi Khan, the Tsangpa forces were defeated in 1642.Following the conflict, the Gelug-led Ganden Phodrang government enforced strict political and religious unification. In 1650, the Fifth Dalai Lama ordered the takeover of Jonang’s administrative seat, Takten Damchöling. By 1658, the monastery was forcibly converted to the Gelug lineage and renamed Ganden Puntsok Ling. The state enacted severe measures: printing blocks for Dölpopa’s Mountain Dharma and other Shentong literature were sealed or burned; study or possession of Shentong texts was outlawed across Central Tibet; and Jonang monastics were forced to convert to the Gelug lineage or flee Central Tibet.Although modern scholars and the 14th Dalai Lama have noted that the suppression was driven primarily by political rivalries rather than pure theological intolerance, the resulting institutional damage was severe. For over three centuries, Western Tibetologists and Central Tibetans assumed that the Jonang school was extinct.However, the tradition survived. Jonang lamas relocated to the remote, isolated regions of eastern Tibet—specifically Amdo, Ngawa, Golok, and northern Sichuan. Centered around Dzamthang Monastery (established in the 15th century), the Jonangpas maintained an unbroken practice lineage.The 19th century Rimé (ecumenical) movement in Kham—led by masters such as Jamgön Kongtrül, Situ Paṇchen, and Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo—revived interest in Jonang literature and Shentong philosophy. Following the Cultural Revolution, previously sealed Jonang printing blocks were uncovered inside Drepung Monastery’s Sixteen Arhats Temple in Lhasa, allowing rare texts by Dölpopa and Tārānātha to be digitized and translated globally.Today, the Jonang tradition operates over sixty active monasteries across eastern Tibet, supporting more than 10,000 monks and nuns. In September 2011, at the 11th Religious Conference in Dharamshala, His Holiness the 14th Dalai Lama and leaders of the Tibetan traditions formally recognized Jonang as an independent, complete fifth major school of Tibetan Buddhism. To formalize this status, the Dalai Lama appointed the late 9th Khalkha Jetsun Dampa of Mongolia (regarded as the reincarnation of Tārānātha) as the spiritual head of Jonang.Despite this spiritual recognition, the Jonang community faces an ongoing political challenge: they remain excluded from formal legislative representation within the Tibetan Parliament-in-Exile. It unites rigorous scholastic philosophy with esoteric tantric practice. Rather than treating Shentong as an abstract theory, Jonang presents it as the direct philosophical description of the contemplative experiences realized through the Kālacakra Six Vajra Yogas. In this framework, when a yogi dissolves all subtle winds into the central channel and stills conceptual thought, the remaining reality is not a void of non-existence, but the unconditioned, radiant essence of Buddha-nature.ConclusionThe Jonang tradition represents one of the most intellectually daring and contemplatively specialized streams of Indo-Tibetan Buddhism. Historically mischaracterized as extinct due to 17th-century political suppression, its survival in eastern Tibet preserved a vital heritage that enriches the broader Buddhist landscape.Through its unique dual identity—as the guardian of the Drō transmission of the Kālacakra completion stage and the primary home of the Shentong Great Mādhyamaka philosophy—Jonang offers a comprehensive model of awakening. It demonstrates that ultimate emptiness (śūnyatā) is not a void of mere absence, but an absolute, unconditioned reality free from all adventitious defilements—a radiant, indwelling potential for liberation present within every living being.
